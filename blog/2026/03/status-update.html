A Pause in the Project

2026-03-15

I've put off writing this post for several weeks now ...

But it's worth saying that the Oils project has been paused for over 2 months, and will continue to be for a few more months.

The reason is that my dad, who is in his late 70's, had a serious stroke on December 28th. He's stable now, but he's been in 4 different medical institutions in the last 10 weeks. The recovery is long and slow.

This event also had a domino effect:

  • He was taking care of my mom, so I'm now juggling two parents. Both physically and virtually (e.g. finances).
  • Real estate changes: finishing up the sale of a townhome, buying a condo, and eventually selling their house.

So this post has some thoughts on what this means for the project.

NLnet Grants

This affects the NLnet grant we're currently working on, and our plans for the future.

Fourth Grant - started June 2025

I'm not sure how long the "pause" will last, but it will likely be past June 2026.

We got the current grant in June 2025, and the grants are typically valid for a year.

So it will be past the end of the current grant, and we won't meet all the milestones. I will e-mail NLnet to try to adjust the milestones, but I'm not sure what will happen.

I'm sorry to all the grant contributors, who did excellent work. I described what we did in December, a few weeks before the stroke:

  • Links to Explain Oils in 2025
  • Oils 0.37.0 - Alpine Linux, YSH, and mycpp

I Was on the Fence About Applying for a 5th Grant

Last year, I wasn't sure if we would apply for a 5th grant. We had been funded since June 2022, and we had basically achieved our funding goals.

That is, OSH, YSH, and mycpp all exist and work. There's a ton of work left to do, but I also wanted to make the project self-sustaining.

But, given the momentum described in the 2 blog posts above, I got pretty excited about applying for a 5th grant:

  • #oils-discuss > Ideas for 5th grant work
  • #oils-discuss > Project Scope: 4th and 5th grant
  • #oils-discuss > What's not in the 4th or 5th grant

Unfortunately, due to my family situation, I can already tell that I won't have time to lead a 5th grant this year.

I'm still interested in the projects we discussed — regtest/debian and a Rust translation — but right now I can only cheer from the sidelines.

Paused Doesn't Mean Unmaintained

I won't be writing much new code in the next few months, but I am working on merging the backlog of pull requests:

Why Merging Takes Time

I'm picky about our code, so the backlog may shrink slowly. I want to keep the code simple, as an axiomatic virtue, and because it allows evolution of OSH into YSH.

So I often make 3 or 4 follow-up commits after contributions. Here's a concrete example:

  • #blog-ideas > Lessons From Improving the Trap Builtin

That is, you can say we do the opposite of "vibe coding where you generate too much code to read".

Current Status

This pause naturally makes me think about the status of the project, and its future. Here is what I think right now ...

OSH

With the fourth grant, we proved that OSH can run decades worth of diverse shell scripts. We started with 131 disagreements on Alpine Linux packages in August, and were down to 12 by November.

I think it's 11 now, and we know the root cause of all the disagreements. See the regtest/aports results, which were the focus of our 4th grant.

And we had many contributors motivated to improve the codebase! In many cases, they taught me things I didn't know — even about "my own" code.

On the other hand, I don't see much evidence that existing distros want to replace their shell with OSH. One thing I've learned is that there's a lot of foundational shell code that's in "maintenance mode". This means that there are few people motivated and skilled enough to replace the shell interpreter that runs it.

I think polishing and documenting our test framework would help, though.

YSH

YSH is pretty solid, and multiple people have each written thousands of lines of YSH. But it has gaps, and there are breaking changes planned.

It does feel like progress on YSH was "paused" while we worked on OSH, which is unfortunate.

However, I appreciate all the feedback on YSH! Discussions often lead to ideas for improvement, and sometimes these ideas aren't to hard to implement. The design is often what takes the longest, and that work happens on Zulip.

Future of the Project

Now what about the future? There are some things I can imagine happening soon, but I also feel like we need some reflection and self-improvement before tackling some of the biggest ideas.

Near Term

The good news is that multiple contributors are interested in the work we had discussed for a 5th grant:

  • A regtest/debian test harness, for Debian packages
    • This is analogous to regtest/aports, our test harness for Alpine Linux packages, which was the focus of the fourth grant.
    • Thanks to Bram for kicking this off! He already found a great bug with sh -c, which I fixed. (It just occured to me that this sh -c quirk may not be desirable in YSH. This is another example of a design bottleneck, i.e. the reason changes are merged at a deliberate pace.)
  • Translating Oils to Rust
    • I mentioned this in December's release announcement
    • Andriy made some progress with re2c --lang rust, and Daveads and Gabe are also interested in working on it.

Because of my family situation, these projects are better thought of as parallel "forks", in different repos, rather than as part of the core project. (This would have been the case with Rust anyway, because I don't have the knowledge to meaningfully review Rust code!)

I mentioned that contributors are of course free to apply for NLnet grants (or other funding) for these projects, or any other projects.

I concede that this could create some chaos for awhile. But I think that open source software is inherently self-directed, and thus chaotic. It's OK if we make mistakes in parallel.

But let's try to coordinate on Zulip, as much as possible. We may not coordinate on the level of detailed code reviews, but we should keep our long term goals, as well as end users, in mind. Again, the design is often the hardest part.

Mini Book

As usual, I wrote notes on Zulip about it:

Project Management: Funding and Leading

Here are some thoughts for myself. They're a bit vague, since I don't know what will happen in the future.

A More Aggressive Funding Strategy?

There's an asymmetry in the project: I have worked on it full-time, but everyone else works on it part-time.

So I'm thinking about ways to fund the project more sustainably. Of course, pretty much every person with an ambitious open source project has thought about this.

And every solution I can think of requires a lot more of my time. That's time that would not be spent designing and implementing Oils.

But, eventually — once my family situation stabilizes — I will have more time.

Get Better at Leading / Management

On the other hand, if we want to be more aggressive about funding, then we have to be able to use the funds effectively. That's not easy.

If I had to give myself an (American-style) grade from A to D on managing the NLnet grants, I'd give myself a B at most. We did achieve our goals, but we could have made better use of contributor energy, perhaps with some more structure.

One thing I started doing was regular office hours via video on Sundays, and I think that was useful. So I'll think about more things like this in the future.

Notes for NLnet

There were many writing milestones on the 4th grant. And I still want to communicate these things:

  • What did we achieve?
    • As mentioned, OSH is in great shape, and there are many people who want to work on it.
    • There are some people "pulling" OSH into distros, which means it's compelling. But perhaps not compelling enough?
    • I'd like to update the graph of progress I published in Oil Is Being Implemented "Middle Out" (March 2022)
  • What did we not achieve?
    • The project is still bottlenecked on design, code review, and possibly other things.
    • One analogy I make is to Nix, which is also funded by NLnet. Even though Nix is open source and partially funded, the creator still felt the need to start a for-profit company. I understand this — it reflects the fact that creating this kind of foundational and principled software is hard, and expensive.

Appendix: The Complexity of Simplicity

I stumbled across this talk by Bryan Cantrill the other day, and it provoked many thoughts related to Oils:

  • The Complexity of Simplicity (youtube.com) - slides (speakerdeck.com)

It talks about software systems along two dimensions:

  • Simple ↔ Complex
  • Emergent ↔ Engineered

These two dimensions create four quadrants. The "emergent and complex" quadrant is called accreted, and that perfectly describes Unix shell, e.g. bash.

To be honest, I'm not sure I agree with all the examples in the rebellious and revolutionary quadrants, and with the distinction itself, but that's not too important here.

I liked the analysis of Unix versus Multics, RISC versus CISC, and NoSQL versus SQL (showing that the argument is subtle).

In any case, I'd like to say that Oils is rebellious or revolutionary! But so far we can only say it's engineered, and YSH will be simple if we can disallow all the legacy.

Watch the talk, and let me know what you think!

The talk also notes that the hardest software to update is the one in our human brains. Learning to write a new shell language like YSH might be an example of that! This brings to mind the mini book I mentioned.
